# Longform profile

Use for a work that asks the reader to stay with an argument, lesson, story, or chapter sequence. The project brief determines whether the work is instructional, narrative, reflective, or something else.

- Give each section a reason to exist in the larger progression. A chapter should develop an idea or situation rather than restart the same introduction.
- Let examples, scenes, and explanations run long enough for their consequences to become visible. Do not replace reasoning with a list of principles.
- Vary pace according to the material. A scene may need texture; a technical explanation may need a compact worked step. Avoid repeated openings and mechanically matched chapter shapes.
- Check transitions and terminology across neighboring sections. Keep purposeful callbacks and remove accidental re-teaching.
- Preserve uncertainty beside the claim it qualifies. Verify sources, quotations, numbers, and chronology against the project's evidence; flag gaps rather than smoothing them away.
- Review the assembled reading order, including front matter, captions, notes, exercises, and endings when present. A good isolated chapter can still fail in sequence.

If the work teaches, check that every required outcome is actually explained or practiced. If the work tells a story, check causality, point of view, character consistency, and what the reader is meant to infer. Neither purpose requires a standard chapter formula.
